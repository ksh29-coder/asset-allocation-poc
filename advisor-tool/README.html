The advisor tool exercise

Four AI models get the same data, the same brief, and ten minutes each. You compare what they built.

Each of the four folders here is a sealed copy of one exercise. A model is given one folder and nothing else. The exercise is not who built the most — it is what each model chose to build, what it refused, and whether the numbers hold up when you check them.

1 · The data they are given

Three files, and nothing else. No internet, no market feed, no benchmark.

The client is invented. The portfolio, the account size and the cost basis are all made up for this exercise. The funds are real JPMorgan ETFs with their real published fees, and the prices are real market history — but no client's data appears anywhere here.

Five years of prices

17,604 daily closing prices covering 27 Aug 2021 to 26 Aug 2026, adjusted for distributions. One detail matters and the models are not warned about it: the funds do not all have five years of history. Several launched in 2023, and one stops five weeks before the rest. A five-year figure is therefore wrong for this client's portfolio, and noticing that is part of the test.

2 · What the models are asked to do

Build a tool that helps a client advisor manage this client's portfolio better.

That is the whole of it. Nothing more specific is coming — working out what "better" means for this client, and what an advisor actually needs in front of them, is the job being handed to you.

A workshop with the advisers produced ten asks.

Nobody has sorted them, costed them, or argued about which matter.

  1. What the client holds today: what it is, and what it costs them in dollars.
  2. How the portfolio has actually performed, and over what period.
  3. What it is concentrated in, and whether that should worry anyone.
  4. The current book compared against an alternative built from the same shelf.
  5. Where the risk actually sits — which holdings drive the volatility.
  6. The worst it has been, and how long it took to recover.
  7. What the client pays in fees over the next fifteen years if nothing changes.
  8. Cheaper ways to hold the same exposure, and what the saving is worth.
  9. What a proposed change costs to execute: turnover, and the gains it realises.
  10. What date range every figure covers, and why that range.

Ten items of this size cannot be built in ten minutes, and the brief says so. Choosing which of them matter is the actual test. Four rules shape the rest:

  • Use only the data you are given. No invented account sizes, yields or rates. Anything resting on an assumption must show that assumption on screen, where the adviser can change it.
  • Prove one number. Put a check on the page that would fail if the arithmetic were wrong — and show the result, including when it fails.
  • The screen is the deliverable. Nobody will read the code, but the numbers it produces will be checked.
  • Ten minutes, self-timed. The model reads the clock itself and stops. Stopping is its job, not ours.

3 · What we are looking for in the results

Not feature count. A model that builds four things it would stake its name on beats one that builds nine it would not.

  • Would you let an adviser take this into a client meeting on Monday? That is the whole test, and you are better placed to answer it than any engineer.
  • What is this client paying? Some tools answer in dollars, some only in percentages. Only one of those answers the question a client actually asks.
  • What did it refuse to build, and do you agree? Declining can be the better answer — ask 4 invites a model to invent an alternative portfolio the data does not support, and refusing that is a mark of judgement, not a gap.
  • Did it find the trade that matters? There are two moves in this book and they are not the same one. The cheap win is obvious. The important one costs real tax. A tool that shows only the first will send an adviser into the meeting recommending the wrong thing.
  • Pick the most impressive number on any screen and ask where it came from. Not all of them can tell you.

4 · How to run it

The folders are empty of code right now. They hold the brief, the data and the installed toolchain — nothing else. There is no run.sh until a model writes one. Run the exercise first, then come back to view the results.

Tell each one to read BRIEF.md and start. Give them nothing else — the brief tells each model that its folder is the entire world. Stop any model still going at ten minutes.

Separate port ranges mean all four can serve at the same time, so nothing collides when you come to look at them.

Viewing the results

Each model writes a SUMMARY.html saying what it built and why, with a link straight through to its own running page. That is the quickest way in — open the four summaries and click through.

If a model's service is not running, start it from its own window:

./run.sh serve                   # prints the URL to open

Resetting for another run

cd advisor-tool
./clean.sh --dry-run             # show what would be removed
./clean.sh                       # remove it, keeping brief, data and node_modules

Marking a run. There is a judge skill in this repo that assesses a completed run — it carries the answer key, the reference figures, and the traps that have separated the field every time. Its first rule is worth knowing even if you mark by hand: never mark from a model's own summary. Every run so far has produced at least one summary that misstates its own work.
